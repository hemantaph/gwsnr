# Summary

Gravitational waves (GWs), ripples in spacetime predicted by Einstein's theory of General Relativity, have revolutionized astrophysics since their first detection in 2015. Emitted by cataclysmic events such as mergers of binary black holes (BBHs), binary neutron stars (BNSs), and black hole-neutron star pairs (BH-NSs), these waves provide a unique window into the cosmos. 

A central quantity in GW analysis is the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), which measures the strength of a GW signal relative to the background noise in detectors such as LIGO (@LIGO:2015, @Abbott:2020, @Buikema:2020), Virgo (@VIRGO:2015, @VIRGO:2019), and KAGRA (@Akutsu:2020, @Aso:2013). While real detections are established using a False-Alarm Rate (FAR) threshold, under stationary Gaussian noise assumptions the condition that the SNR exceeds a chosen threshold can serve as a practical proxy (@Essick:2023, @Essick:2024), especially in simulations of detectable events and in studies aimed at extracting astrophysical information (@Abbott:2016:detection).

Applications such as population simulations for rate estimation (@Abbott:2016:rates) and hierarchical Bayesian inference with selection effects (@Thrane:2019, @Essick:2024) require repeated and efficient computation of the Probability of Detection ($P_{\rm det}$), which is generally derived from SNR. However, traditional approaches that rely on noise-weighted inner products for SNR evaluation are computationally demanding and often impractical for such large-scale analyses (@Taylor:2018, @Gerosa:2020).

# Statement of need

The `gwsnr` Python package is a `ler`-affiliated tool designed to provide efficient and flexible resources for computing the optimal signal-to-noise ratio ($\rho_{\rm opt}$) and estimating the probability of detection ($P_{\rm det}$). It is intended for LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) analyses and for astrophysics researchers working on population studies and simulations. This includes tasks such as hierarchical Bayesian inference with selection effects and astrophysical rate estimation. `gwsnr` addresses the needs of researchers working with large simulated populations, where repeated SNR and $P_{\rm det}$ calculations can become computationally expensive. Unlike broader gravitational-wave analysis packages such as `Bilby` [@bilby] and `PyCBC` [@pycbc], `gwsnr` focuses specifically on efficient detectability calculations for population-level studies.

For these large-scale simulations, `gwsnr` estimates $P_{\rm det}$ by evaluating $\rho_{\rm opt}$, or a statistically modelled observed SNR $\rho_{\rm obs}$ based on $\rho_{\rm opt}$, against a detection-statistic threshold under flexible detector, waveform, and population settings. Additionally, the package provides tools to estimate this threshold from injection catalogues, following [@Essick:2023]. $\rho_{\rm opt}$ depends on the intrinsic and extrinsic source parameters, the detector antenna response ($F_{+,\times}$), and the noise power spectral density [@Allen:2012]. The package provides a flexible and user-friendly interface for combining detector noise models, waveform families, detector configurations, and signal parameters. It accelerates $\rho_{\rm opt}$ evaluation using several specialized methods, such as partial-scaling interpolation, multiprocess inner-products, and artificial neural networks (more methods are detailed in the software design section). Furthermore, the package computes the horizon distance ($D_{\rm hor}$), which is a standard measure of detector sensitivity, via both analytical [@Allen:2012] and numerical methods. Although intended for various research purposes, `gwsnr` has been primarily used with `ler` in the selection of simulated detectable strongly lensed gravitational-wave events and the calculation of rates [@ler:2024; @Leo:2024; @More:2025]. Its speed and flexibility allow for rapid calculations under diverse astrophysical settings that would otherwise be computationally prohibitive.

# State of the field

In gravitational-wave data analysis, commonly used packages such as `Bilby` [@bilby], `PyCBC` [@pycbc], and `GstLAL` [@GstLAL], operating alongside `LALSuite` [@lalsuite:2018], provide high-precision tools for Bayesian inference, matched-filter searches, signal processing, and waveform generation. While these packages are widely adopted, they are not primarily designed for fast, repeated evaluation of $\rho_{\rm opt}$ and $P_{\rm det}$ across large simulated populations, where direct noise-weighted inner-product calculations become a significant computational bottleneck. To address this, related studies have developed machine-learning methods to estimate detectability. For example, Gerosa et al. [@Gerosa:2020] trained classifiers using optimal-SNR thresholds, while Callister et al. [@Callister:2024] trained an emulator directly from search-pipeline detections defined by false-alarm-rate criteria. Similarly, Chapman-Bird et al. [@ChapmanBird:2023] implemented an SNR-based selection model for extreme mass ratio inspirals where detectability is defined by a threshold crossing of a noise-realized SNR drawn from a non-central $\chi^2$ distribution, relying on neural networks because direct parameter-space interpolation was restricted by high dimensionality. While these machine-learning methods provide rapid evaluation, their applicability depends on specific training data, detector configurations, and waveform models. Errors are heavily concentrated near the sharp detection boundary, where minor variations can misclassify marginal events [@Gerosa:2020]. Additionally, emulators like the one by Callister et al. [@Callister:2024] are tied to the specific pipeline configurations and injection campaigns used for training. Modifying detector noise curves, networks, waveform families, or threshold criteria therefore requires computationally expensive dataset regeneration and complete model retraining.


The `gwsnr` package addresses this need by providing a modular and SNR-based framework for efficient detectability calculations. In `gwsnr`, the optimal SNR, $\rho_{\rm opt}$, is computed explicitly through accelerated pathways, while the observed SNR, or matched-filter SNR, $\rho_{\rm obs}$, can be modelled statistically from $\rho_{\rm opt}$ under a chosen stationary-noise model. The detection threshold for $\rho_{\rm obs}$ can also be estimated from injection catalogues using empirical sensitivity information, following [@Essick:2023]. This framework provides flexibility within a single interface, allowing researchers to switch between different evaluation pathways, such as partial-scaling interpolation, neural network approximations, and full multiprocessing inner-product integration, depending on their needs for accuracy, speed, and waveform model. The build-versus-contribute justification is therefore based on the need for a dedicated, adaptable library for repeated SNR and $P_{\rm det}$ calculations with user-controlled detector and waveform settings. By working with established C-based waveform infrastructure such as `LALSuite` and JAX-based waveform tools such as `ripplegw` [@Edwards:2023], `gwsnr` fills a specific role as a fast detectability engine for larger simulation pipelines such as `ler` [@ler:2024].

# Software design

The design of `gwsnr` is based on the trade-off between accuracy, speed, and waveform generality. Direct noise-weighted inner-product calculations are accurate and general, but they are expensive when repeated over large simulated populations. Partial-scaling interpolation is much faster, but it is mainly valid for non-precessing binaries. ANN-based methods provide rapid estimates for more general waveform settings, but their accuracy depends on the training data. For this reason, `gwsnr` provides several calculation paths under a common interface, with inputs given by the detector PSD, antenna response, waveform model, detector network, source parameters, and detection threshold.

The baseline calculation follows the standard frequency-domain **inner product** [@Allen:2012],

$$
\langle a | b \rangle =
4 \Re \int_{f_{\min}}^{f_{\max}}
\frac{\tilde{a}(f)\tilde{b}^*(f)}{S_n(f)} df ,
$$

where $S_n(f)$ is the detector PSD. The optimal SNR is $\rho=\sqrt{\langle h|h\rangle}$. For the two polarizations, $h_+$ and $h_\times$,

$$
\rho =
\sqrt{
F_+^2 \langle \tilde{h}_+|\tilde{h}_+\rangle
+
F_\times^2 \langle \tilde{h}_\times|\tilde{h}_\times\rangle
}.
$$

This path is retained because it supports the widest range of waveform models, including spin-precessing systems and waveforms with subdominant modes. `gwsnr` accelerates it using `multiprocessing`, `numba.njit` for antenna-pattern generation, and optional `jax` backends with `ripplegw` for waveform generation [@Edwards:2023].

For non-spinning and aligned-spin binaries, `gwsnr` implements a faster **partial-scaling interpolation** method based on the FINDCHIRP scaling [@Allen:2012]. The method precomputes the partial-scaled SNR,

$$
\rho_{1/2}
=
\frac{D_\mathrm{eff}}{\mathcal{M}^{5/6}}
\rho_{\rm opt},
$$

where $\mathcal{M}$ is the chirp mass and $D_{\rm eff}$ is the effective distance. The quantity $\rho_{1/2}$ is stored on a parameter grid, with a two-dimensional grid for non-spinning binaries and a four-dimensional grid for aligned-spin binaries. New SNR values are recovered by interpolation and rescaling,

$$
\rho_{\rm opt}
=
\rho_{1/2}
\frac{\mathcal{M}^{5/6}}{D_\mathrm{eff}} .
$$

This avoids repeated inner-product integrations by interpolating only the expensive mass- and spin-dependent part of the calculation. The interpolation uses multidimensional irregular grids with local cubic-Hermite splines. The full $\rho_{\rm opt}$ generation, including antenna-pattern calculation and luminosity-distance scaling, is wrapped in just-in-time compiled functions for efficient parallel execution on CPUs and supported GPUs. For randomly sampled parameters, this method reaches an accuracy of $\gtrsim 99.5\%$, with maximum absolute error $\lesssim 0.6$ and median absolute error $\sim 0.002$, while generating up to $10^6$ $\rho_{\rm opt}$ values in $\lesssim 200$ ms.

As a supplementary path, `gwsnr` includes **ANN-based** $\rho_{\rm opt}$ and $P_{\rm det}$ estimation using `tensorflow` [@tensorflow:2015] and `scikit-learn` [@scikitlearn:2011]. This path is useful for waveform settings where partial-scaling interpolation is not directly applicable, such as spin-precessing waveforms with subdominant modes. The model uses partial-scaled SNR quantities to reduce the input dimensionality from 15 to 5. A pretrained model is provided for IMRPhenomXPHM with O4 design sensitivity, and users can train their own models for different detector, waveform, and population settings.

`gwsnr` also provides a **hybrid SNR recalculation** method. It first estimates SNRs using partial scaling or ANN prediction, identifies events close to the threshold $\rho_{\rm th}$, and recalculates those systems using the direct inner-product method. This keeps the speed of approximate methods while improving reliability near the detection boundary, where small SNR errors can directly affect $P_{\rm det}$. In benchmark tests with randomly distributed parameters, the hybrid method reaches an accuracy of $\gtrsim 99.9\%$ while avoiding direct inner-product calculations for the full population.

The **probability of detection** is based on a threshold for the observed matched-filter SNR, $\rho_{\rm obs,thr}$. In the Gaussian approximation, $\rho_{\rm obs}$ is modelled as a normal random variable centred on $\rho_{\rm opt}$, or on $\rho_{\rm opt,net}$ for a detector network, with unit variance [@Fishbach:2020; @Abbott:2019]. In the non-central $\chi$ model, the squared observed SNR follows a non-central $\chi^2$ distribution. For a single detector,

$$
\rho_{\rm obs}^2
\sim
\chi^2_{\rm nc}
\left(
k=2,
\lambda=\rho_{\rm opt}^2
\right),
$$

and for a network of $N$ detectors,

$$
\rho_{\rm obs,net}^2
\sim
\chi^2_{\rm nc}
\left(
k=2N,
\lambda=\rho_{\rm opt,net}^2
\right).
$$

The package uses `scipy.stats.ncx2` [@scipy:2020] for this calculation and supports threshold estimates from injection catalogues following [@Essick:2023]. Users can also compute custom thresholds from their own catalogues, including parameter-dependent thresholds such as functions of source-frame primary mass, $m_{\rm 1,src}$.

Finally, `gwsnr` computes the horizon distance, $D_{\rm hor}$, defined as the maximum distance at which an optimally oriented source can be detected for a chosen threshold [@Allen:2012]. The analytical method rescales a known effective distance,

$$
D_{\rm hor}
=
\frac{\rho_{\rm opt}}{\rho_{\rm th}} D_{\rm eff}.
$$

The numerical method maximizes the SNR over sky location and solves for the luminosity distance, $d_L$, where

$$
\rho(d_L) - \rho_{\rm opt,thr} = 0 .
$$


# Research impact statement

The `gwsnr` package provides the speed and accuracy needed for detectability calculations in large simulated populations of compact binary mergers. By making repeated signal-to-noise ratio and detection-probability calculations computationally practical, it supports population-level simulations where detectability must be evaluated across millions of pre-generated events. These applications include astrophysical rate estimation, detector-sensitivity studies, and selection-effect modelling in hierarchical Bayesian inference. A primary realized impact of `gwsnr` is its integration as the core detectability calculator within the `ler` package [@ler:2024]. In this role, it evaluates $P_{\rm det}$ to identify detectable unlensed and strongly lensed gravitational-wave events and to calculate their occurrence rates, supporting population-level lensing analyses in published studies [@Janquart:2023; @Leo:2024; @More:2025].

The community readiness of `gwsnr` is supported by its formal peer review within the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) Scientific Collaboration [@lerlvkpnpreview:2024], active maintenance through GitHub issues, and use in collaboration communication channels such as LIGO Mattermost. The package is publicly available on the Python Package Index (PyPI), where it has recorded more than 400 downloads [@gwsnrpypi:2026]. Its documentation provides the underlying theory, examples, and benchmarks needed to reproduce the main calculations [@gwsnrdocs:2026]. Together, these provide evidence that `gwsnr` is usable beyond a single analysis and can support independent simulation pipelines with custom detector, waveform, threshold, and population settings.
